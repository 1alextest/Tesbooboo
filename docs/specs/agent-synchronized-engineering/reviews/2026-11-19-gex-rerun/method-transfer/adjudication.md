# Independent adjudication of responses K and M

## Scope and criteria

This assessment uses exactly four supplied documents: `input/rehearsal-packet-covers.md` (“Cover brief”), `input/interactive-fiction-scene-review.md` (“Scene brief”), and `adjudication-input/response-k.md` and `response-m.md` (“K” and “M”). The briefs were read and the criteria derived before the responses were considered. No guidance, metadata, other review, implementation, external source, or live system was inspected.

The Cover brief requires a modest plan that reuses the specified application capabilities. Its binding outcome is exactly one cover followed by every approved source page, preserving order, page properties, fonts, content, and the unchanged original file. All three cover values must be reproduced exactly; missing values block completion. Librarian authority constrains coordinator changes. Assessment must distinguish the conforming Cedar example from Birch’s prohibited 98% content scaling, even though their page counts and boxes match. Undecided printing mode affects physical handling, not the specified PDF sequence. [Cover brief, “Authority and required behavior,” “Existing capabilities,” and “Examples accompanying the request.”]

The Scene brief requires a bounded passage-review plan using existing selection, canon, history, and comment capabilities. Approved canon controls literal staging; brainstorming and old drafts cannot override it. Writers retain revisions and the owner retains canon approval. The review must distinguish Amber’s contradiction from Blue’s permissible opening, offer evidence-supported emotional possibilities, and keep the unresolved sender-reveal objective separate from C19’s statement about earlier audience knowledge. Assessments should check attribution, usefulness, permissible alternatives, and limits without claiming observed reader or product outcomes. [Scene brief, “Authority and boundaries,” “Existing capabilities,” and “One fact we do not have.”]

These criteria do not require a particular model, architecture, cover design, emotional reading, or overall winner.

## Response K: rehearsal covers

K provides a complete, proportionate plan: select the approved source, populate its approved title, collect exact ensemble/date text, validate required fields, render one cover, prepend it through the preserving assembler, preview, save the manifest, and download. Its responsibility table distinguishes existing capabilities from wiring, template, validation, and manifest work. It proposes checking the renderer’s actual interface during implementation rather than claiming inspected code. Regenerating against the original source explicitly prevents accumulated covers. [K, “Proposed user flow” and “Existing responsibilities and necessary work.”]

Its preservation reasoning is materially correct. Birch fails because scaling changes protected score content; unchanged boxes do not establish unchanged content. The proposed verification compares copied pages with independent source expectations and distinguishes harmless serialization differences from prohibited changes. Requiring the retained original bytes to stay unchanged does not become an impossible requirement that the assembled document equal the source file byte for byte. [K, “What the two manifests establish” and “Acceptance and falsification plan”; Cover brief, required behavior.]

K also accepts a different cover-only layout and does not require normalized source-page properties. Its simplex/duplex discussion correctly treats ordinary printing as conditional and refuses to insert a blank under the current contract. Asking about the first score page’s preferred physical side is useful elicitation, not a new completion prerequisite. [K, “Material open question: physical printing.”]

No material unsupported product claim appears. The overflow handling and optional digest/reference are additional design proposals, not established capabilities or requirements. Their implementation cost is unverified. “Correction” of an overlong value could be clearer about respecting the approved title, but the preceding instruction explicitly prohibits changing that title through cover revision. This ambiguity does not overturn the plan’s authority boundary.

## Response M: scene review

M is materially correct on the same staging and authority questions. Its concrete Amber observation cites C17/C18 and explains why literal direction forecloses an interpretive escape. Blue satisfies those supplied constraints, and suggesting Blue’s staging does not choose the final scene. Old drafts and the unapproved note remain subordinate context. [M, “Concrete review of the two passages.”]

M’s interpretation table makes each candidate reading inspectable by pairing wording with an explicit inference limit. It also states two valuable scope distinctions: C19 constrains the audience’s previous knowledge, not necessarily the characters’ knowledge; and a selected excerpt cannot establish everything disclosed by the end of a larger scene. The conditional reveal/nonreveal assessments remain conditional until the owner decides. [M, “Shared continuation: supported possibilities, with limits” and “Material unresolved question: audience knowledge by scene end.”]

Its flow and capability table reuse comments, paragraph identity, approved canon, revision history, and local persistence. Keeping observations tied to the assessed revision addresses stale findings without inventing a new canon store. Unlike K, M defers choosing the review mechanism until the output contract is agreed. This is permissible for the requested limited planning exercise, though implementation estimation still needs a mechanism and feasibility decision. Naming a “review operation” alone does not establish that generating reliable observations is already available. M does not claim otherwise. [M, “Writer flow” and “Existing capability and necessary change.”]

Its verification plan covers determinate canon outcomes, source authority, missing evidence, persistence/editability, state preservation, and revision changes. The proposed writer/reader exercise appropriately limits interpretive findings to the sampled readers and passage. It demonstrates an assessment design, not actual usefulness. [M, “Evidence plan and acceptance checks.”]

## Comparative judgment and limits

The artifacts support local differences, not a material superiority finding. K offers a more concrete candidate review mechanism, an availability checkpoint, and a manual/assisted usefulness comparison. M more explicitly distinguishes character knowledge from audience knowledge, passage scope from whole-scene disclosure, and PDF preservation from printer scaling. These differences could matter to particular planning audiences; the documents do not establish their practical benefit or cost.

Both responses refer to guidance outside this assessment’s permitted evidence. K includes named guidance citations and a methodological preamble; M references guidance in its opening and closing. Their claims about applying that guidance cannot be verified here and receive no evidential credit. Their substantive recommendations can nevertheless be checked independently against the two briefs, where they largely agree. Methodological framing itself is not evidence of better planning or successful transfer.

The frozen judgment is that both responses materially satisfy both synthetic briefs, preserve legitimate alternatives and unresolved decisions, and propose useful, bounded verification. Residual implementation choices and minor wording qualifications do not establish a decisive correctness gap. This assessment establishes document-grounded compliance and relative specificity only: it provides no live product result, reader study, causal explanation of quality differences, or evidence of general effectiveness across products.
